What firms do when they can stop disclosing

Evidence from the GHGRP reporting thresholds, 2010–2023  ·  Elaine  ·  28 July 2026

US environmental regulation contains a provision that lets a facility stop disclosing its emissions once it has stayed small for long enough. This is a first look at whether firms use it — and at what that reveals about the private cost of public disclosure. Two of the three things I expected to find are not in the data; the third turned out sharper than I anticipated.

01The institutional detail that does the work

A reporting threshold with an exit ramp

GHGRP requires facilities emitting ≥25,000 tCO₂e to report, and EPA publishes the reports facility by facility. Less well known is 40 CFR 98.2(i): a facility already in the program may stop reporting after five consecutive years below 25,000, or three consecutive years below 15,000, on notifying EPA — and must resume if emissions ever reach 25,000 again.

That provision converts a static threshold into a dynamic rule with two waiting periods, and it makes the avoidance decision a dated, observable event rather than a shape in a histogram. It is the reason the third hypothesis below survives when the first two do not.

02What I expected to find, and did not

The density is smooth at both thresholds

Hypothesis 1 — not identified

Facilities below 25,000 do not report at all, so the sample is truncated exactly at the cutoff. The centre panel shows a sharp discontinuity there; the right panel, where the rule does not bind, runs smoothly through. The jump is real but mechanical — there is no bunching estimate to be had at 25,000, and a histogram that looks like evidence would be an artefact.

Hypothesis 2 — rejected

I had hoped 15,000 would be a clean interior notch: crossing it buys exit in three years instead of five, both sides are observed, so the counterfactual density is estimable in the standard Chetty–Kleven way. There is no visible excess mass there. Facilities are not manipulating reported emissions to reach either off-ramp.

03What the data does show

They are not managing emissions — they are managing when they leave

Hypothesis 3 — supported

Facility-years eligible to leave under 98.2(i) end in exit 21.0% of the time; ineligible ones, 1.5%. That is 996 exits out of 4,755 eligible facility-years — a factor of fourteen, and not a thin-tail curiosity.

04The sharp test

Excess mass at exactly the statutory waiting periods

For every facility that stops reporting, I counted the consecutive years it spent below each threshold immediately before leaving. If the rule drives exit, the count should pile up at exactly 3 for the 15,000 route and exactly 5 for the 25,000 route — and at nothing in particular for thresholds that carry no rule at all.

05A large confound, found and handled

The 2015 spike is the oil price collapse, not disclosure avoidance

Exits spike sharply in 2015 — 756 against 90–260 in a typical year. Before trusting anything above I had to explain it. I worked through three candidates:

  • A bug in my loader. EPA changed the workbook layout in 2016, splitting oil and gas into separate sheets; facilities that merely moved sheets would look like exits. Ruled out — only 4 of the 756 appear anywhere in the 2016 workbook.
  • A rule change. EPA did finalise amendments effective 2016, but they revised methods and confidentiality determinations rather than removing this population from coverage.
  • An economic shock. Of the 756, 580 report under subpart C alone; the NAICS composition is oil and gas extraction (334), mining support (101), utilities (89), pipelines (84); the states are Texas, Arkansas, Oklahoma, Colorado, Wyoming; median emissions 24,409.

It is the 2014–16 oil price collapse. Production fell, emissions fell below the threshold, facilities left. A large share of my exit variation is driven by the oil cycle rather than by anything about disclosure.

The right-hand block of the table above is the check: dropping those sectors entirely and every 2015 exit, the result holds at +178% and +195%, placebos still null.

06Where this leaves me

Bunching below 15,000excess mass at the 3-year off-ramp density smooth; no manipulation of levels
Exit responds to the ruleplausible yes, and sharply — 21.0% vs 1.5%

The surviving result is about timing, not levels. Facilities do not manage emissions to reach the off-ramp, but conditional on becoming eligible they leave at fourteen times the base rate, and they leave at precisely the moment the statute allows. That reframes the question from what do firms do to escape disclosure to how quickly do they take the exit once the rule permits it — a weaker claim, but one the data supports.

07What is still weak

  • The always-covered group is not a clean placebo for this particular test. It shows elevated mass at 3 and 5 years for every threshold including the placebos — an artefact of shorter panels and smaller n. The credible placebo here is a different threshold within the same facilities, which is what the table uses.
  • The 100,000 placebo in the restricted sample has n=99 and a confidence interval running to +8. It is uninformative rather than reassuring.
  • "Exit" is currently inferred from disappearance from the panel. EPA collects an actual Notification to Discontinue Reporting; I should verify against it rather than infer.
  • Tomar (2023, JAR) finds GHGRP disclosure caused a 7.9% emissions reduction through peer benchmarking. If that is operating it pushes facilities toward the threshold for reasons unrelated to avoidance, and I have not yet separated the two channels.

08Open questions

  • Is exit timing enough to carry a paper on its own? It gives a revealed-preference measure of the private cost of public disclosure, but it is a duration result, not a price.
  • How do I map excess mass in waiting time into a cost? Kleven's chapter handles notches in a static density; I have not worked out whether the same logic transfers.
  • I want to test whether exit accelerates after the June 2017 Paris withdrawal announcement, with eligibility as the exposure margin. But the 2015 oil shock sits immediately before — and the Clean Power Plan regulated exactly the always-covered group, so CPP and my comparison group are the same firms. Whether the Paris announcement alone carries enough variation is unresolved.
  • Does TRI or NEI contain a comparable cessation provision? A second setting would make this a study of disclosure thresholds generally rather than of one EPA rule.
